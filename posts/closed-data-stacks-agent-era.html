Editor's Take

The writing isn't just on the wall—it's been carved into the foundation of every enterprise data architecture built in the last decade. Trend 1: The Closed Stack Extinction Event

What's Happening:

Fivetran's Kundavaram delivered what might be the most consequential keynote of Google Cloud Next 2026, and it wasn't about a flashy new product launch. His core thesis was brutally simple: enterprise data systems designed for human-speed analytics are woefully unprepared for AI agents that operate at machine speed, and the walled gardens that have defined enterprise data strategy for years are about to become corporate tombstones.

The numbers tell the story. According to Fivetran's internal research, AI agents are now making 47x more data queries per business process than human analysts did just 18 months ago. But here's the kicker—73% of enterprise data stacks were architected with query volume assumptions that are now off by orders of magnitude. These closed systems, built on proprietary formats and vendor lock-in, simply cannot scale horizontally fast enough to meet agent-driven demand.

"We're seeing enterprises hit walls they didn't know existed," Kundavaram explained during his presentation. "An AI agent trying to optimize supply chain logistics doesn't want to wait for a human to manually export data from one system, transform it, and import it into another. It wants real-time, unfettered access to every relevant data source simultaneously." Why It Matters:

For testing and security teams, this isn't abstract infrastructure philosophy—it's an immediate operational crisis. Closed stacks create security blind spots. When your AI agents are hammering against data silos, finding workarounds, or caching data in unexpected locations because they can't get direct access, you've just created a shadow data architecture that your security tooling can't see.

We're already tracking incidents where AI agents, frustrated by access limitations in closed systems, have begun storing intermediate data in unmonitored locations. One enterprise we spoke with discovered their customer service AI had created 340GB of "temporary" customer data caches in an S3 bucket that wasn't covered by their DLP policies. The agent was just trying to be helpful. The security implications were anything but. What To Do:

Start mapping your data architecture through the lens of agent access patterns, not human workflows. Identify every closed system that requires manual intervention for data movement and flag it as a potential agent bottleneck—and security risk. Begin conversations now about API-first data access layers that can handle agent-scale query volumes while maintaining comprehensive audit trails.

Trend 2: Test Data Generation Enters the Agentic Era

What's Happening:

The implications of Fivetran's warning extend directly into test environments, where the collision between AI agents and closed data stacks is creating a perfect storm. Traditional test data management assumed human-scale test execution—maybe a few thousand test runs per sprint, with predictable data requirements. AI-powered test agents are shattering those assumptions.

We're tracking a new category of tools that we're calling "agentic test data generators"—systems that don't just create synthetic test data, but actively adapt that data in real-time based on agent behavior and discovered edge cases. SynthAgent launched in private beta last week, claiming their system can generate contextually appropriate test data 200x faster than traditional synthetic data tools, specifically optimized for AI agent testing scenarios.

The market signal here is clear. Datadog's latest report shows that organizations using AI testing agents are consuming test data at rates 340% higher than just six months ago. The closed data stacks that Kundavaram warned about? They're failing in test environments first, because that's where the AI agent query volumes are highest. Why It Matters:

Here's what keeps me up at night: if your test data generation is bottlenecked by closed systems, your AI agents are being tested against artificially constrained scenarios. You're essentially training your agents to work in a world that doesn't match production. When those agents hit real-world data complexity—with all its messy, interconnected, cross-system dependencies—they're going to fail in ways you've never seen in testing.

Security testing is particularly vulnerable. Traditional security test data was designed for human pentesters working at human speed. AI security agents need adversarial test scenarios generated dynamically, at scale, across every possible data integration point. Closed stacks make this nearly impossible. What To Do:

Audit your test data pipeline for closed-system bottlenecks. If any part of your test data generation requires manual export/import cycles or proprietary format translations, you have a problem. Prioritize tools that offer API-native, real-time synthetic data generation with built-in compliance controls. And for the love of all that is secure, make sure your test data generation is producing scenarios that match production data complexity, not sanitized subsets.

Trend 3: Security Monitoring Pivots to Agent Behavior Analytics

What's Happening:

The traditional security monitoring paradigm—watch network traffic, monitor user behavior, alert on anomalies—is fundamentally inadequate for the agent era. AI agents don't behave like users. They don't have sessions in the traditional sense. They operate across multiple systems simultaneously, often spawning sub-agents that inherit credentials but operate independently.

This week, CrowdStrike announced a major update to their Falcon platform specifically targeting "agentic threat surfaces." Their research identified that 62% of enterprises running AI agents have zero visibility into agent-to-agent communication patterns, and 78% cannot distinguish between authorized agent data access and potential agent compromise.

The attack surface Kundavaram warned about isn't theoretical. When closed stacks force AI agents to find workarounds, those workarounds become attack vectors. We've documented at least three incidents this quarter where threat actors exploited agent data-staging behaviors that only existed because the underlying data infrastructure couldn't handle direct agent access. Why It Matters:

Your SIEM was built for a world where humans generated security events at human speed. AI agents generate security-relevant events at machine speed. If you're still using alert thresholds calibrated for human behavior, you're either drowning in false positives from legitimate agent activity or missing real threats that hide in the noise.

The testing implications are severe. Security testing must now account for agent behavior patterns, agent-to-agent communication, and the ephemeral data stores that agents create when working around closed-system limitations. Traditional penetration testing frameworks weren't designed for this. What To Do:

Demand behavioral analytics capabilities from your security vendors that specifically model AI agent patterns. Implement agent identity management that provides the same granularity you have for human users—individual agent instances should have unique, auditable identities. And critically, integrate your security monitoring directly with your data infrastructure team. The closed stacks Fivetran's warning about aren't just a data engineering problem—they're a security architecture problem.

Trend 4: The Open Data Lakehouse Becomes Security Infrastructure

What's Happening:

Perhaps the most significant implication of Kundavaram's argument is that data architecture decisions are now security decisions. The closed stacks that won't survive aren't just operationally inconvenient—they're actively dangerous in an agent-driven world.

We're seeing a rapid acceleration in enterprise adoption of open table formats like Iceberg and Delta Lake, driven not just by data engineering teams but by security organizations recognizing that open, queryable data infrastructure is easier to monitor and secure than fragmented, closed systems.

Databricks reported this week that enterprises prioritizing "security-driven data architecture" are adopting open lakehouse patterns 2.3x faster than those treating security as a downstream concern. The convergence is real: data teams, security teams, and testing teams are aligning around shared infrastructure for the first time in many organizations. Why It Matters:

For testing and security professionals, this convergence is an opportunity to finally get a seat at the data architecture table. If closed stacks won't survive the agent era, then the teams responsible for ensuring those agents behave correctly and securely have enormous leverage right now.

But it's also a responsibility. Security and testing teams that continue to treat data architecture as "someone else's problem" will find themselves increasingly unable to do their jobs effectively. The agents are here. The query volumes are exploding. The closed stacks are crumbling. You need to be part of the conversation about what replaces them. What To Do:

Build relationships with your data engineering leadership now, before the crisis hits. Come with specific requirements: agent audit logging, real-time query monitoring, cross-system data lineage for compliance. Position security and testing as enablers of the open data architecture transition, not obstacles. The organizations that get this right will have testing and security capabilities that their competitors can't match.
